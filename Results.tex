With the theoretical framework for gravitational lensing 
of GWs now established, including the geometric optics 
formalism, the signatures of strong lensing on GW signals, 
and the joint PE approach that exploits lensed image pairs to improve sky localization, we are in a position to present the original results of this thesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} describes the simulation setup and the strongly lensed BBH population generated with \textsc{LeR}. Chapter~\ref{Parameter_estimation_pipeline} presents the PE pipeline. Each image is first analyzed individually with \textsc{bilby}, and the two images of each event are then analyzed jointly with \textsc{hanabi}, which coordinates the coherent analysis of the image pair, sharing the intrinsic binary parameters between images while sampling the lensing parameters, on top of the \textsc{bilby} inference backend. The chapter also describes the automation infrastructure deployed on the Picasso HPC cluster. Finally, Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} presents the sky localization results, comparing the 90\% credible sky area obtained from single-image and joint analyses across the final set of analyzed events.

\chapter{Simulation setup and implementation}
\label{ch:simulation}

This chapter describes the construction of the lensed 
BBH population used as input to the PE pipeline. The simulation proceeds in two stages: first, a large Monte Carlo (MC) population of strongly lensed BBH events is generated with \textsc{LeR} v0.4.2 
\cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}, and then a realistic detectability filter is applied to select the subset of events that would be observed by the H1 and L1 detectors under O5 sensitivity conditions.

\section{Lensed BBH population with \textsc{LeR}}
\label{sec:LER_population}

\textsc{LeR} (LVK Event Rate calculator) is a 
Python framework for the joint simulation and rate 
computation of unlensed and strongly lensed GW 
populations \cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}. It integrates source and lens population sampling, lens equation solving via \textsc{lenstronomy} \cite{lenstronomy}, and detector selection into a single reproducible pipeline. All lensing calculations are performed in the geometric optics regime and under the thin lens approximation, both of which are valid for galaxy-scale lenses in the LIGO frequency band (see \S\ref{sec:lensing_intro}).

The simulation is initialized with the following 
configuration, specifying the detector network, 
noise curves, and waveform approximant:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
ler = LeR(
    npool=6,
    event_type="BBH",
    ifos=['L1', 'H1'],
    psds={'L1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt',
          'H1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt'},
    waveform_approximant="IMRPhenomXPHM",
    spin_zero=False,
    spin_precession=True,
)
\end{lstlisting}

Here, \texttt{npool} sets the number of parallel worker 
processes used for the MC sampling and SNR 
calculations. The simulation was run in a Jupyter 
notebook on the CIT JupyterHub, using the \texttt{igwn} 
conda environment (Python 3.11.14) distributed via 
CVMFS, with 48 CPU cores available. The \texttt{event\_type="BBH"} argument selects the BBH source population model described in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}, as opposed to the BNS or NS-BH options also supported by \textsc{LeR}. The \texttt{ifos} argument specifies the detector network over which the network SNR $\rho_\mathrm{net}$ is computed, here restricted to the two LIGO detectors, H1 and L1.

The \texttt{psds} argument sets the noise curve assigned to each detector, here the O5a design sensitivity PSD \cite{O5PSD}. This choice is motivated by the goal of simulating a realistic future observing scenario. The default PSD in \textsc{LeR} approximates O4 sensitivity, but no strongly lensed events were detected in O4. Simulating at O5a sensitivity instead, the first epoch of the O5 run with a BNS range of 225\,Mpc \cite{O5PSD}, provides a more informative and forward-looking setting for this study. Figure~\ref{fig:psd_O5a} shows the projected O5a strain sensitivity curve used in this work, compared against the L1 O4 sensitivity achieved in September 2024 and the later O5b and O5c projections.

The \texttt{waveform\_approximant} argument selects 
\texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} \cite{IMRPhenomXPHM} for the SNR 
computation, which incorporates spin precession and 
higher-order multipole moments. Finally, \texttt{spin\_zero=False} enables non-zero component spins in the source population, and \texttt{spin\_precession=True} allows for spin tilts misaligned with the orbital angular momentum, so that the resulting waveforms include precession effects. Both are necessary for the spin parameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params} to be sampled rather than fixed to zero.

The population is generated by drawing events in 
batches of 20\,000 until 500 strongly lensed 
detectable events are collected, each requiring 
that at least two images independently satisfy 
$\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
lensed_params = ler.selecting_n_lensed_detectable_events(
    size=500,
    batch_size=20000,
    snr_threshold=10,
    num_img=2,
    resume=False,
    output_jsonfile="n_lensed_params_bbh.txt",
)
\end{lstlisting}

The SNR threshold of $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$ and 
the requirement of at least two detectable images follow standard detection criteria adopted in GW searches, so that the resulting population reflects events that a real detector network could plausibly identify and confidently claim as lensed, rather than a theoretical population extending down to marginal or sub-threshold significance.

A total of approximately 152\,000 candidate events 
were drawn to collect the 500 detectable ones, 
yielding a total lensed event rate of 
$\mathcal{R}_L \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
for this detector configuration and sensitivity. 
This value is consistent with early forecasts for 
second-generation detector networks at design-like 
sensitivity, which span roughly $0.1$-$10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
depending on the assumed high-redshift merger rate and 
detector network \cite{Wierda_2021}, and is somewhat lower than forecasts for the full three- or four-detector network, reflecting the more limited H1+L1 configuration adopted here.


\section{Injected parameter distributions}
\label{sec:injected_params}

The 500 events produced by \textsc{LeR} represent the strongly lensed population detectable under ideal observing conditions. To obtain a more realistic injection set, a duty cycle filter is applied: for each image of each event, both H1 and L1 are independently activated with probability 
$p_\mathrm{det} = 0.70$, reflecting typical O4/O5 
single-detector duty cycles \cite{GWTC5}. An image is 
retained only if both detectors are simultaneously active and $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$. Events with fewer than two surviving images are discarded; when more than two images survive, only the two with the highest network SNR are kept. After applying this filter once to the fixed 500-event 
population, 157 events are selected as the final injection set analyzed with \textsc{bilby}.

\chapter{Parameter estimation pipeline}
\label{Parameter_estimation_pipeline}

\section{Pipeline overview}
\label{sec:pipeline_overview}

The PE pipeline processes each of the 157 injected events through the same sequence of automated stages, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_diagram}. Starting from the filtered population \texttt{n\_lensed\_params\_bbh\_filtered.txt} produced in \S\ref{sec:injected_params}, a single script, \texttt{Generator\_inis\_lenskyloc.py}, generates for every event the two single-image \texttt{.ini} configurations, the joint \texttt{.ini} configuration, and the companion SLURM scripts needed for the downstream steps described below, populating priors and injection parameters directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} output (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}).

Each single-image \texttt{.ini} file is analyzed independently with \textsc{bilby\_pipe}, using the lensed waveform source model provided by \textsc{hanabi} (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}). This yields, for every event, two independent posterior distributions, one per image. Once both single-image jobs have completed, the joint \texttt{.ini} file is submitted to \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), which coordinates a coherent analysis of the image pair, sharing the intrinsic source parameters and sky location between images while sampling the lensing parameters, on top of the same \textsc{bilby} inference backend used for the single-image runs.

From this point the pipeline branches into two independent post-processing tracks. In the first, the joint posterior is merged into a lightweight result file and passed, together with the single-image data realizations, to \texttt{hanabi\_postprocess\_result}, which recomputes the per-image and network signal-to-noise ratios consistently across the single and joint analyses; a dedicated script then extracts these recovered SNRs into a per-event summary table (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}), later combined across all events for the SNR consistency checks of \S\ref{sec:parameter_recovery}. In the second track, the single-image and joint posteriors are combined with \textsc{PESummary} \cite{TODO:pesummary} into a single metafile, from which sky maps are generated for each image and for the joint analysis using \texttt{ligo-skymap-from-samples}, \texttt{ligo-skymap-stats}, and \texttt{ligo-skymap-constellations}, providing the 90\% credible sky areas compared between single-image and joint analyses in Chapter~\ref{Skyloc}.

The entire pipeline, from \texttt{.ini} generation to job submission and the SNR post-processing track, is automated on the Picasso HPC cluster; the sky-map track is run separately, as described in \S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}.

\section{Single-image analysis with \textsc{bilby}}
\label{sec:bilby_config}

Each of the two images of every event is first analyzed independently with \textsc{bilby\_pipe} \cite{BILBY}, treating it as an isolated signal characterized by its own data segment, noise realization, and set of extrinsic parameters. The \texttt{.ini} configuration is generated automatically for each image from a shared template (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}); the key data-conditioning and sampler settings are as follows:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
detectors=[H1, L1]
sampling-frequency=2048
reference-frequency=20
minimum-frequency={'H1': 20, 'L1': 20, 'waveform': 13.33}
maximum-frequency={'H1': 896, 'L1': 896}
psd-dict={"H1": "T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt",
          "L1": "T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt"}
\end{lstlisting}

The same O5a PSD used to generate the population with \textsc{LeR} (\S\ref{sec:LER_population}) is adopted for the analysis, so that the noise assumptions are consistent between simulation and recovery. Unlike the data duration, which is fixed for the whole population in \textsc{LeR}, the segment \texttt{duration} used by \textsc{bilby\_pipe} is computed individually for every image from its injected component masses and spins, using \textsc{LALSimulation} bounds on the merger and ringdown time together with the expected waveform length at $f_\mathrm{low}=20$\,Hz, rounded up to the next power of two. This keeps each analysis as short as the signal allows without truncating the inspiral, rather than using a single conservative duration for the whole population.

The injected and recovered waveforms both use the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} approximant \cite{TODO:IMRPhenomXPNR}, rather than the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} approximant used by \textsc{LeR} to generate the population and compute detection SNRs (\S\ref{sec:LER_population}). This is a deliberate choice rather than an oversight: \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} is computationally cheap and sufficiently accurate for the population-level detectability calculation performed by \textsc{LeR}, whereas \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} incorporates numerical-relativity-tuned precession effects and is the more accurate, PE-oriented model of the two. Using it for parameter estimation follows standard practice in recent LVK analyses, at the cost of a waveform mismatch between the population-generation and inference stages that should be kept in mind when interpreting parameter recovery (\S\ref{sec:parameter_recovery}).

The waveform is generated through \textsc{hanabi}'s lensed source model, \texttt{hanabi.lensing.waveform.strongly\_lensed\_BBH\_waveform} \cite{TODO:hanabi}, which implements the lensed strain expression of \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}, combining the intrinsic \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} waveform with an image-dependent amplitude and Morse phase factor. In the single-image configuration this factor is fixed to the injected image type rather than treated as a free parameter,

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
prior-dict={..., image_type = 1.0}
\end{lstlisting}

since a single lensed image is fully degenerate with an unlensed signal of appropriately rescaled amplitude and shifted phase: the magnification and Morse phase cannot be separated from the ordinary distance and coalescence-phase parameters using one image alone. Breaking this degeneracy is precisely the role of the joint analysis (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}).

The remaining priors follow standard choices for BBH parameter estimation: a chirp mass prior adapted individually for each event to a $\pm30\%$ range around its injected value (rather than a single wide range for the whole population, which would waste live points on regions of parameter space far from every event's true chirp mass), mass ratio and component-mass constraints, uniform spin magnitudes and precession angles, an isotropic sky location, and a $50$-$20\,000$\,Mpc uniform luminosity distance prior. Sampling is performed with \texttt{dynesty} \cite{MCMC_vs_NS} using the acceptance-walk method,

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
n-parallel=3
sampler-kwargs={'nlive': 1000, 'naccept': 60,
                 'sample': 'acceptance-walk', 'npool': 128}
\end{lstlisting}

with three independent \texttt{dynesty} runs combined per image (\texttt{n-parallel=3}) to mitigate sampler-to-sampler variance, and $128$ parallel workers per run.


\section{Joint analysis with \textsc{hanabi}}
\label{sec:hanabi_joint}

Once both single-image configurations exist, the joint analysis is performed with \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} \cite{TODO:hanabi}, which coherently analyzes the two images of an event as two triggers of the same underlying source rather than as independent signals:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
n-triggers=2
trigger-ini-files=[single_event_xxx_image1.ini,
                    single_event_xxx_image2.ini]

common-parameters=[chirp_mass, mass_ratio, mass_1, mass_2,
                    a_1, a_2, tilt_1, tilt_2, phi_12, phi_jl,
                    cos_theta_jn, ra, dec, psi, phase]
\end{lstlisting}

Each trigger inherits its own data segment, noise realization, and PSD from its single-image \texttt{.ini} file, but the parameters listed in \texttt{common-parameters}, namely the intrinsic binary parameters, sky location, polarization, and coalescence phase, are constrained to be identical between the two images, reflecting the fact that both are gravitationally lensed copies of the same source. Notably, \texttt{luminosity\_distance} is deliberately left out of this shared list: each image retains its own independent effective distance parameter, so that the relative magnification between images is recovered through the ratio of the two independently fitted distances rather than sampled as a separate parameter. This is why the lensing-specific parameters reduce to the image type alone,

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
lensing-prior-dict={relative-magnification^(1): 1,
    image_type^(1) = hanabi.lensing.prior.DiscreteUniform(
        name='image_type^(1)', minimum=1, N=3),
    relative_magnification^(2) = 1,
    image_type^(2) = hanabi.lensing.prior.DiscreteUniform(
        name='image_type^(2)', minimum=1, N=3)}
\end{lstlisting}

with the relative magnification fixed to unity (its physical content already captured by the two independent distance posteriors) and each image's Morse-index class sampled independently from the three discrete types introduced in \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}. Sharing the sky location across two images observed at different times, typically separated by minutes to months (Figure~\ref{fig:timedelay_hist}), is the key mechanism behind the improvement in sky localization: the Earth's rotation between the two epochs changes the detectors' antenna pattern for the same fixed $(\alpha,\delta)$, effectively lengthening the interferometric baseline available for triangulation (\S\ref{sec:joint_PE_skyloc}).

The joint likelihood is evaluated over both images simultaneously, using the same sampler settings as the single-image runs (\texttt{dynesty}, \texttt{nlive=1000}, \texttt{naccept=60}, acceptance-walk, \texttt{n-parallel=3}), but with substantially higher resource requirements, reflecting the added cost of a two-detector, two-image coherent likelihood:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
request-memory=180.0
scheduler-analysis-time=71:30:00
\end{lstlisting}

compared to \texttt{request-memory=110.0} and \texttt{scheduler-analysis-time=48:00:00} for each single-image run.


\section{Automation and infrastructure on Picasso}
\label{sec:automation_picasso}

The full pipeline, from the filtered injection set to the final SNR tables, is automated for all 157 events on the Picasso HPC cluster (SLURM scheduler), with jobs executed directly inside a dedicated \texttt{conda}/\texttt{miniforge} environment (\texttt{hanabi\_bilby260}), without containerization.

\subsection*{Configuration and script generation}

A single script, \texttt{Generator\_inis\_lenskyloc.py}, converts the filtered injection catalogue \texttt{n\_lensed\_params\_bbh\_filtered.txt} into a complete, ready-to-submit run directory for every event. For each event it: (i) computes the true mass ratio, chirp mass, and a per-event data duration from the injected masses and spins (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}); (ii) adapts the chirp-mass prior to a $\pm30\%$ range around the injected value; (iii) populates the \texttt{injection-dict} of both single-image templates directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} parameters, mapping, e.g., \texttt{effective\_luminosity\_distance} to \texttt{luminosity\_distance} and \texttt{effective\_geocent\_time} to \texttt{geocent\_time}; and (iv) writes out the two single-image \texttt{.ini} files, the joint \texttt{.ini} file, and the companion SLURM scripts for the downstream post-processing chain (final-result merging, joint SNR post-processing, sky map generation), substituting the event-specific paths into a set of shared templates. This guarantees that the 157 event directories are internally consistent and reproducible, and that no configuration is edited by hand.

\subsection*{Submission and job dependencies}

Job submission is split into two stages, reflecting the pipeline's dependency structure. The first stage, \texttt{submit\_all\_part1.sh}, loops over all event directories and, for each one, submits the two single-image \texttt{bilby\_pipe} jobs and then the joint \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} job with an explicit SLURM dependency on both single-image job IDs,

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
sbatch --dependency=afterok:$jid_s1:$jid_s2 $joint_script
\end{lstlisting}

so that the joint analysis for an event is only launched once both of its single-image analyses have completed successfully. The second stage, \texttt{submit\_all\_part2.sh}, runs after the first stage completes and chains, again via \texttt{afterok} dependencies, the merging of the joint posterior into a lightweight result file (\texttt{bilby\_result}, capped at $20\,000$ samples), the \textsc{hanabi} post-processing step (\texttt{hanabi\_postprocess\_result}, which recomputes per-image SNRs from the joint posterior using the noise realizations generated for the single-image analyses), and the final SNR extraction (\texttt{compute\_snr.py}).

Post-processing splits into the two tracks introduced in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}. The SNR-recovery track, final-result merging, \texttt{hanabi\_postprocess\_result}, and SNR extraction, is fully automated on Picasso as part of \texttt{submit\_all\_part2.sh}. The sky-map track, generating \textsc{PESummary} pages and sky maps for every event, is instead run on the CIT cluster using HTCondor, within the \texttt{igwn} \textsc{conda} environment distributed via CVMFS: \texttt{Pesummaries\_automated\_condor\_all.py} and \texttt{Skymap\_condor.py} are each launched as an HTCondor job array of 157 processes, indexed automatically by \texttt{\$(Process)}, generating the summary pages and sky maps for all events in parallel. The parameter estimation itself, both single-image and joint, was always run on Picasso and never on CIT; only this post-processing track uses HTCondor, since the version of \textsc{summarypages} available in the Picasso environment produced errors, while the \texttt{igwn} environment on CIT ran it correctly.

\subsection*{Compute losses}

The joint analysis is the most expensive step of the pipeline, requiring up to $71.5$\,h of wall time per event (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}). Partway through the production run, the Picasso allocation available for this project was reduced to a $24$\,h wall-time limit, below what the remaining joint jobs required. The last seven joint analyses were interrupted mid-run when the allocation was exhausted; rather than repeatedly resubmit and monitor these jobs against an uncertain future allocation, the analysis was finalized with the $150$ events that had completed successfully. This loss of $7$ out of $157$ events ($157\to150$) is a consequence of the compute allocation available at the time, not a scientific selection criterion, and does not bias the final sample in any systematic way tied to source or lens parameters.
