With the theoretical framework for gravitational lensing 
of GWs now established, including the geometric optics 
formalism, the signatures of strong lensing on GW signals, 
and the joint PE approach that exploits lensed image pairs to improve sky localization, we are in a position to present the original results of this thesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} describes the simulation setup and the strongly lensed BBH population generated with \textsc{LeR}. Chapter~\ref{Parameter_estimation_pipeline} presents the PE pipeline. Finally, Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} presents the sky localization results, comparing the 90\% credible sky area obtained from single-image and joint analyses across the final set of analyzed events.

\chapter{Simulation setup and implementation}
\label{ch:simulation}

This chapter describes the construction of the lensed 
BBH population used as input to the PE pipeline. The simulation proceeds in two stages: first, a large Monte Carlo (MC) population of strongly lensed BBH events is generated with \textsc{LeR} v0.4.2 
\cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}, and then a realistic detectability filter is applied to select the subset of events that would be observed by the H1 and L1 detectors under O5 sensitivity conditions.

\section{Lensed BBH population with \textsc{LeR}}
\label{sec:LER_population}

\textsc{LeR} (LVK Event Rate calculator) is a 
Python framework for the joint simulation and rate 
computation of unlensed and strongly lensed GW 
populations \cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}. It integrates source and lens population sampling, lens equation solving via \textsc{lenstronomy} \cite{lenstronomy}, and detector selection into a single reproducible pipeline. All lensing calculations are performed in the geometric optics regime and under the thin lens approximation, both of which are valid for galaxy-scale lenses in the LIGO frequency band (see \S\ref{sec:lensing_intro}).

The simulation is initialized with the following 
configuration, specifying the detector network, 
noise curves, and waveform approximant:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
ler = LeR(
    npool=6,
    event_type="BBH",
    ifos=['L1', 'H1'],
    psds={'L1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt',
          'H1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt'},
    waveform_approximant="IMRPhenomXPHM",
    spin_zero=False,
    spin_precession=True,
)
\end{lstlisting}

Here, \texttt{npool} sets the number of parallel worker 
processes used for the MC sampling and SNR 
calculations. The simulation was run in a Jupyter 
notebook on the CIT JupyterHub, using the \texttt{igwn} 
conda environment (Python 3.11.14) distributed via 
CVMFS, with 48 CPU cores available. The \texttt{event\_type="BBH"} argument selects the BBH source population model described in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}, as opposed to the BNS or NS-BH options also supported by \textsc{LeR}. The \texttt{ifos} argument specifies the detector network over which the network SNR $\rho_\mathrm{net}$ is computed, here restricted to the two LIGO detectors, H1 and L1.

The \texttt{psds} argument sets the noise curve assigned to each detector, here the O5a design sensitivity PSD \cite{O5PSD}. This choice is motivated by the goal of simulating a realistic future observing scenario. The default PSD in \textsc{LeR} approximates O4 sensitivity, but no strongly lensed events were detected in O4. Simulating at O5a sensitivity instead, the first epoch of the O5 run with a BNS range of 225\,Mpc \cite{O5PSD}, provides a more informative and forward-looking setting for this study. Figure~\ref{fig:psd_O5a} shows the projected O5a strain sensitivity curve used in this work, compared against the L1 O4 sensitivity achieved in September 2024 and the later O5b and O5c projections.

The \texttt{waveform\_approximant} argument selects 
\texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} \cite{IMRPhenomXPHM} for the SNR 
computation, which incorporates spin precession and 
higher-order multipole moments. Finally, \texttt{spin\_zero=False} enables non-zero component spins in the source population, and \texttt{spin\_precession=True} allows for spin tilts misaligned with the orbital angular momentum, so that the resulting waveforms include precession effects. Both are necessary for the spin parameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params} to be sampled rather than fixed to zero.

The population is generated by drawing events in 
batches of 20\,000 until 500 strongly lensed 
detectable events are collected, each requiring 
that at least two images independently satisfy 
$\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
lensed_params = ler.selecting_n_lensed_detectable_events(
    size=500,
    batch_size=20000,
    snr_threshold=10,
    num_img=2,
    resume=False,
    output_jsonfile="n_lensed_params_bbh.txt",
)
\end{lstlisting}

The SNR threshold of $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$ and 
the requirement of at least two detectable images follow standard detection criteria adopted in GW searches, so that the resulting population reflects events that a real detector network could plausibly identify and confidently claim as lensed, rather than a theoretical population extending down to marginal or sub-threshold significance.

A total of approximately 152\,000 candidate events 
were drawn to collect the 500 detectable ones, 
yielding a total lensed event rate of 
$\mathcal{R}_L \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
for this detector configuration and sensitivity. 
This value is consistent with early forecasts for 
second-generation detector networks at design-like 
sensitivity, which span roughly $0.1$-$10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
depending on the assumed high-redshift merger rate and 
detector network \cite{Wierda_2021}, and is somewhat lower than forecasts for the full three- or four-detector network, reflecting the more limited H1+L1 configuration adopted here.


\section{Injected parameter distributions}
\label{sec:injected_params}

The 500 events produced by \textsc{LeR} represent the strongly lensed population detectable under ideal observing conditions. To obtain a more realistic injection set, a duty cycle filter is applied: for each image of each event, both H1 and L1 are independently activated with probability 
$p_\mathrm{det} = 0.70$, reflecting typical O4/O5 
single-detector duty cycles \cite{GWTC5}. An image is 
retained only if both detectors are simultaneously active and $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$. Events with fewer than two surviving images are discarded; when more than two images survive, only the two with the highest network SNR are kept. After applying this filter once to the fixed 500-event 
population, 157 events are selected as the final injection set analyzed with \textsc{Bilby}.

\section{Pipeline overview}
\label{sec:pipeline_overview}


This chapter presents the PE pipeline built on the injection set described in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation}, together with the automation infrastructure used to run it for all 157 events. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_diagram} places this pipeline within the full analysis developed in this thesis, from the population generation of Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} (Stage~1) to the sky localization results of Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} (Stage~4). The rest of this section focuses on Stages~2 and~3: the automated generation of \textsc{Bilby}/\textsc{Hanabi} configurations, and the single-image plus joint PE itself.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/diagrama_pipeline_reduced_bo.pdf}
    \caption{Overview of the full analysis pipeline developed in this thesis. Stage~1 (\S\ref{sec:LER_population}-\S\ref{sec:injected_params}): the lensed BBH population is generated with \textsc{LeR} and reduced from 500 detectable events to the final injection set of 157 through the duty cycle and SNR selection process (Table~\ref{tab:selection_funnel}). Starting from the filtered population \texttt{n\_lensed\_params\_bbh\_filtered.txt} produced in \S\ref{sec:injected_params} (Stage~1), a single script, \texttt{Generator\_inis\_lenskyloc.py}, generates for every event the two single-image \texttt{.ini} configurations, the joint \texttt{.ini} configuration, and the \href{https://slurm.schedmd.com/overview.html}{SLURM} scripts needed for the steps described below, populating priors and injection parameters directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} output (Stage~2, \S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}).

Each single-image \texttt{.ini} file is analyzed independently with \texttt{bilby\_pipe} \cite{bilby_pipe_paper}, using the lensed waveform source model provided by \textsc{Hanabi} (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}). This yields, for every event, two independent posterior distributions, one per image. Once both single-image jobs have completed, the joint \texttt{.ini} file is submitted with \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), which jointly analyzes the image pair, sharing the intrinsic source parameters and sky location between images while sampling the lensing parameters, on top of the same \textsc{Bilby} inference backend used for the single-image runs.

From this point the pipeline branches into two independent post-processing tracks. In the first, the joint posterior is merged into a lightweight result file and passed, together with the single-image data realizations, to \texttt{hanabi\_postprocess\_result}, which recomputes the per-image and network SNRs consistently across the single and joint analyses; a dedicated script then extracts these recovered SNRs into a summary table for each event, later combined across all events for the SNR consistency checks of \S\ref{sec:parameter_recovery}. In the second track, the single-image and joint posteriors are combined with \textsc{PESummary} \cite{PESummary} into a single metafile, from which sky maps are generated for each image and for the joint analysis using \texttt{ligo-skymap-from-samples}, \texttt{ligo-skymap-stats}, and \texttt{ligo-skymap-constellations}, providing the 90\% credible sky areas and the constellation localization compared between single-image and joint analyses in Chapter~\ref{Skyloc}.

The entire pipeline, from \texttt{.ini} generation to job submission and the SNR post-processing track, is automated on the \href{https://www.scbi.uma.es/web/es/supercomputacion/\#documentation}{Picasso HPC cluster} (SLURM scheduler); the sky-map track is run separately, as described in \S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}.

The three parallel runs (\texttt{n-parallel}) are independent full \textsc{Dynesty} analyses combined into a single posterior to mitigate sampler-to-sampler variance, while \texttt{npool} parallelizes the likelihood evaluation within each individual run and matches the number of CPUs requested per SLURM job.

The same O5a PSD used to generate the population with \textsc{LeR} (\S\ref{sec:LER_population}) is adopted for the analysis, so that the noise assumptions are consistent between simulation and recovery. Unlike the data duration, which is fixed for the whole population in \textsc{LeR}, the segment duration adopted for each image is computed individually from its injected component masses and spins, using \textsc{LALSimulation}~\cite{lalsuite} bounds on the merger and ringdown time together with the expected waveform length at $f_{\min}=20$\,Hz, rounded up to the next power of two. This keeps each analysis as short as the signal allows without truncating the inspiral, rather than using a single conservative duration for the whole population.

The injected and recovered waveforms both use the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} approximant \cite{IMRPhenomXPNR}, rather than the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} approximant used by \textsc{LeR} to generate the population and compute the detection SNRs that define the selection process of \S\ref{sec:injected_params}. This choice is deliberate: \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} is computationally cheap and sufficiently accurate for the population-level detectability calculation performed by \textsc{LeR}, whereas \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} incorporates precession effects calibrated against NR simulations and is the more accurate of the two, better suited for PE, following standard practice in recent LVK analyses. For the parameter ranges considered here, the two models are expected to agree closely, so this choice between the population-selection and PE stages is not expected to introduce a significant waveform-systematics effect.

The waveform is generated through \textsc{Hanabi}'s lensed source model, \href{https://github.com/ricokaloklo/hanabi/blob/master/hanabi/lensing/waveform.py}{\texttt{hanabi.\allowbreak lensing.\allowbreak waveform.\allowbreak strongly\_lensed\_BBH\_waveform}}, which implements the lensed strain expression of \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}, combining the intrinsic \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} waveform with an image-dependent amplitude and Morse phase factor. In the single-image configuration this factor is fixed to \texttt{image\_type=1} (Type I, a minimum of the Fermat potential) rather than treated as a free parameter. 

Type I images carry no Morse phase shift ($n=0$, \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}), so their waveform is identical in phase to that of an ordinary unlensed signal; the only lensing imprint, an amplitude rescaling and a shifted arrival time, is already absorbed into the standard \texttt{luminosity\_distance} and \texttt{geocent\_time} parameters. The single-image prior fixes \texttt{image\_type} to this value regardless of the true injected type, since doing so does not significantly bias the recovery of the remaining parameters. Each image is thus analyzed as if it were an ordinary CBC signal; identifying the true image type of each image, together with the relative magnification between them, is instead the specific role of the joint analysis (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), which samples \texttt{image\_type} independently for each image from a discrete prior.

The remaining priors follow standard choices for BBH PE: a chirp mass prior adapted individually for each event to a $\pm30\%$ range around its injected value (rather than a single wide range for the whole population, which would waste live points on regions of parameter space far from every event's true chirp mass), mass ratio and component-mass constraints, uniform spin magnitudes and precession angles, an isotropic sky location, and a $50$-$20\,000$\,Mpc uniform luminosity distance prior.



\section{Joint analysis with \textsc{Hanabi}}
\label{sec:hanabi_joint}

As introduced in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}, the joint analysis is performed with \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe}, treating the two images of an event as two triggers of the same underlying source rather than as independent signals, with each trigger pointing to its corresponding single-image \texttt{.ini} file and otherwise inheriting its own data segment, noise realization, and PSD. The parameters shared between the two images are the intrinsic binary parameters (masses and spins), the sky location, the polarization angle, and the coalescence phase, reflecting the fact that both images are gravitationally lensed copies of the same source. The luminosity distance, by contrast, is fitted independently for each image.

Formally, \textsc{Hanabi}'s lensing model requires specifying both a relative magnification and an image type for each image. Table~\ref{tab:hanabi_lensing_priors} summarizes these lensing-specific priors: the relative magnification is fixed to $1$ for both images (not sampled), since its physical content is already captured by the two independently fitted luminosity distances discussed above, while the image type is the only genuinely free lensing parameter, sampled independently for each image from a discrete uniform prior over the three Morse-index classes introduced in \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}.

Sharing the sky location across two images observed at different times, typically separated by minutes to months (Figure~\ref{fig:timedelay_hist}), is the key mechanism behind the improvement in sky localization: the Earth's rotation between two arrival times changes the detectors' antenna pattern for the same fixed $(\alpha,\delta)$, effectively acting as a longer effective arm separation for triangulation (\S\ref{sec:joint_PE_skyloc}).

The joint likelihood is evaluated over both images simultaneously, using the same sampler settings as the single-image runs (Table~\ref{tab:bilby_settings}), but with substantially higher resource requirements, reflecting the added cost of a coherent likelihood evaluated across two detectors and two images: $180$\,GB of memory and a $71.5$\,h wall-time limit, compared to $110$\,GB and $48$\,h for each single-image run.


\section{Automation and infrastructure on Picasso}
\label{sec:automation_picasso}

Running this pipeline for all 157 events relies on two components: a script that generates every configuration and job file automatically, and a two-stage submission mechanism that enforces the job dependencies of \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}. Jobs execute directly inside a dedicated \texttt{conda}/\texttt{miniforge} environment (\href{https://github.com/XxavierGarcia/TFM_reproducibility_material/blob/main/environment_full.yml}{\texttt{hanabi\_bilby260}}) on Picasso, whose exact specification is provided for reproducibility.

\subsection{Configuration and script generation}

Beyond the duration and chirp-mass prior already computed for each event (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}), \texttt{Generator\_\allowbreak inis\_\allowbreak lenskyloc.py} populates the \texttt{injection-dict} of both single-image templates directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} parameters, mapping, e.g., \texttt{effective\_\allowbreak luminosity\_\allowbreak distance} (the magnification-rescaled apparent distance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:apparent_DL}) to \texttt{luminosity\_\allowbreak distance}, and \texttt{effective\_\allowbreak geocent\_\allowbreak time} (the lensed arrival time of Eq.~\eqref{eq:apparent_tc}) to \texttt{geocent\_time}, so that each image is injected with its own apparent extrinsic parameters rather than the source's true values. This guarantees that all 157 event directories are internally consistent and reproducible, with no configuration edited by hand; Appendix~\ref{implementataion_pipeline} reproduces a representative single-image and joint \texttt{.ini} pair.

\subsection{Submission and job dependencies}

Job submission is split into two stages. The first, \texttt{submit\_all\_part1.sh}, submits the two single-image \texttt{bilby\_pipe} jobs for each event, followed by the joint \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} job with an explicit SLURM dependency on both single-image job IDs,

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
sbatch --dependency=afterok:$jid_s1:$jid_s2 $joint_script
\end{lstlisting}

The second stage, \texttt{submit\_all\_part2.sh}, runs after the first stage completes and chains, again via \texttt{afterok} dependencies, the SNR-recovery track described in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}.

The sky-map track, generating \textsc{PESummary} pages and sky maps for every event, is instead run on \href{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/guide/computing-centres/cit/}{LIGO's computing cluster at Caltech (CIT)} using \href{https://htcondor.readthedocs.io/en/latest/}{HTCondor}~\cite{HTCondor}, within the \href{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/conda/}{IGWN Conda Distribution}: \texttt{Pesummaries\_automated\_condor\_all.py} and \texttt{Skymap\_condor.py} are each launched as an HTCondor job array of 157 processes, generating the summary pages and sky maps for all events in parallel. The PE itself, both single-image and joint, was always run on Picasso and never on CIT; only this post-processing track uses HTCondor, since the version of \textsc{PESummary} available in the Picasso environment produced errors, while the \texttt{igwn} environment on CIT ran it correctly. The complete pipeline code, including both the Picasso and CIT branches, is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/XxavierGarcia/TFM_reproducibility_material}.

\subsection{Incomplete events}

The joint analysis is the most expensive step of the pipeline, requiring up to $71.5$\,h of wall time per event (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}). Partway through the production run, the Picasso allocation available for this project was reduced to a $24$\,h wall-time limit, below what the remaining joint jobs required. The last seven joint analyses\footnote{Events \texttt{inj110}, \texttt{inj115}, \texttt{inj118}, \texttt{inj137}, \texttt{inj138}, \texttt{inj142}, and \texttt{inj150}.} were interrupted mid-run when the project's compute allocation was exhausted; the next allocation period was not due to begin for approximately two months, and rather than delay the analysis by that much to recover seven events, it was finalized with the $150$ events that had completed successfully. This loss of $7$ out of $157$ events is a consequence of the compute allocation available at the time, not a scientific selection criterion, and does not bias the final sample in any systematic way tied to source or lens parameters.
