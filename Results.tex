With the theoretical framework for gravitational lensing 
of GWs now established, including the geometric optics 
formalism, the signatures of strong lensing on GW signals, 
and the joint PE approach that exploits lensed image pairs to improve sky localization, we are in a position to present the original results of this thesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} describes the simulation setup and the population of strongly lensed BBH events generated with \textsc{LeR}. Chapter~\ref{Parameter_estimation_pipeline} presents the PE pipeline. Each image is first analyzed individually with \textsc{Bilby}, and the two images of each event are then analyzed jointly with \textsc{Hanabi}, which combines them through a coherent likelihood that shares their parameters (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), on top of the \textsc{Bilby} inference backend. The chapter also describes the automation infrastructure deployed on the Picasso HPC cluster, and validates the recovered parameters against the injected values, including a correction for the magnification-distance degeneracy and a consistency check of the lensed hypothesis via the coherence ratio. Finally, Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} presents the sky localization results, comparing the 90\% credible sky area obtained from single-image and joint analyses across the final set of analyzed events.

\chapter{Simulation setup and implementation}
\label{ch:simulation}

This chapter describes the construction of the lensed 
BBH population used as input to the PE pipeline. The simulation proceeds in two stages: first, a large Monte Carlo (MC) population of strongly lensed BBH events is generated with \textsc{LeR} v0.4.2 
\cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}, and then a realistic detectability filter is applied to select the subset of events that would be observed by the H1 and L1 detectors under O5 sensitivity conditions.

\section{Lensed BBH population with \textsc{LeR}}
\label{sec:LER_population}

\textsc{LeR} (LVK Event Rate calculator) is a 
Python framework for the joint simulation and rate 
computation of unlensed and strongly lensed GW 
populations \cite{phurailatpam2026lerlvkligovirgokagracollaboration}. It integrates source and lens population sampling, lens equation solving via \textsc{Lenstronomy} \cite{lenstronomy}, and detector selection into a single reproducible pipeline. All lensing calculations are performed in the geometric optics regime and under the thin lens approximation, both of which are valid for galaxy-scale lenses in the LIGO frequency band (see \S\ref{sec:lensing_intro}).

The simulation is initialized with the following 
configuration, specifying the detector network, 
noise curves, and waveform approximant:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
ler = LeR(
    npool=6,
    event_type="BBH",
    ifos=['L1', 'H1'],
    psds={'L1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt',
          'H1': 'T2500310-v2_O5a_strain_psd.txt'},
    waveform_approximant="IMRPhenomXPHM",
    spin_zero=False,
    spin_precession=True,
)
\end{lstlisting}

Here, \texttt{npool} sets the number of parallel worker 
processes used for the MC sampling and SNR 
calculations. The \texttt{event\_type="BBH"} argument selects the BBH source population model described in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}, as opposed to the BNS or NS-BH options also supported by \textsc{LeR}. The \texttt{ifos} argument specifies the detector network over which the network SNR $\rho_\mathrm{net}$ (\S\ref{sec:matched_filtering}) is computed, here restricted to the two LIGO detectors, H1 and L1. Throughout this thesis, every quoted SNR, whether computed by \textsc{LeR} for the injected population or recovered from the \textsc{Bilby}/\textsc{Hanabi} posteriors (\S\ref{sec:parameter_recovery}), is the optimal SNR $\rho_\mathrm{opt}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:optimal_SNR}, evaluated directly from a template and the detector PSD, rather than the noise-dependent matched-filter SNR of Eq.~\eqref{eq:SNR_MF} that would be obtained by filtering real, noisy data.

The \texttt{psds} argument sets the noise curve assigned to each detector, here the O5a design sensitivity PSD \cite{O5PSD}. This choice is motivated by the goal of simulating a realistic future observing scenario. The default PSD in \textsc{LeR} approximates O4 sensitivity, but no strongly lensed events were detected in O4. Simulating at O5a sensitivity instead, the first epoch of the O5 run with a BNS range of 225\,Mpc \cite{O5PSD}, provides a more informative and forward-looking setting for this study. Figure~\ref{fig:psd_O5a} shows the projected O5a strain sensitivity curve used in this work, compared against the L1 O4 sensitivity achieved in September 2024 and the later O5b and O5c projections.

The \texttt{waveform\_approximant} argument selects 
\texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} \cite{IMRPhenomXPHM} for the SNR 
computation, which incorporates spin precession and 
higher-order multipole moments. The population presented here uses the original release of this model, with no additional \texttt{waveform-arguments-dict} options passed to \textsc{LeR}. A more recent variant \cite{Colleoni:2024knd} refines the treatment of precession and the final-spin prescription through additional arguments,
\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
waveform-approximant = IMRPhenomXPHM
waveform-arguments-dict = {"PhenomXHMReleaseVersion": 122022,
                            "PhenomXPFinalSpinMod": 2,
                            "PhenomXPrecVersion": 320}
\end{lstlisting}
but this improved configuration was not used for the population generation in this work. Finally, \texttt{spin\_zero=False} enables non-zero component spins in the source population, and \texttt{spin\_\allowbreak precession=\allowbreak True} allows for spin tilts misaligned with the orbital angular momentum, so that the resulting waveforms include precession effects. Both are necessary for the spin parameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:LER_params} to be sampled rather than fixed to zero.

The population is generated by drawing events in 
batches of 20\,000 until 500 strongly lensed 
detectable events are collected, each requiring 
that at least two images independently satisfy 
$\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=numpyStyle]
lensed_params = ler.selecting_n_lensed_detectable_events(
    size=500,
    batch_size=20000,
    snr_threshold=10,
    num_img=2,
    resume=False,
    output_jsonfile="n_lensed_params_bbh.txt",
)
\end{lstlisting}

The SNR threshold of $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$ and 
the requirement of at least two detectable images follow standard detection criteria adopted in GW searches, so that the resulting population reflects events that a real detector network could plausibly identify and confidently claim as lensed, rather than a theoretical population extending down to marginal or sub-threshold significance.

A total of approximately 152\,000 candidate events 
were drawn to collect the 500 detectable ones, 
yielding a total lensed event rate of 
$\mathcal{R}_L \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
for this detector configuration and sensitivity. 
This value is consistent with early forecasts for 
second-generation detector networks at design-like 
sensitivity, which span roughly $0.1$-$10\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ 
depending on the assumed high-redshift merger rate and 
detector network \cite{Wierda_2021}, and is somewhat lower than forecasts for the full three- or four-detector network, reflecting the more limited H1+L1 configuration adopted here.


\section{Injected parameter distributions}
\label{sec:injected_params}

The 500 events produced by \textsc{LeR} represent the strongly lensed population detectable under ideal observing conditions. To obtain a more realistic injection set, a duty cycle filter is applied: for each image of each event, both H1 and L1 are independently activated with probability $p_\mathrm{det} = 0.70$, reflecting typical O4 single-detector duty cycles \cite{GWTC5}.

An image is retained only if both detectors are simultaneously active and $\rho_\mathrm{net} \geq 10$. Events with fewer than two surviving images are discarded; when more than two images survive, only the two with the highest network SNR are kept. After applying this filter once to the fixed 500-event population, 157 events are selected as the final injection set analyzed with \textsc{Bilby}. Table~\ref{tab:selection_funnel} summarizes the successive stages of this selection process, from the initial MC draws performed by \textsc{LeR} to this final injection set.

The time delay $\Delta t$ between lensed images is the second key ingredient of the joint analysis, since it sets the rotation angle of the Earth (and hence the change in the detector antenna pattern) between successive image arrivals. Figure~\ref{fig:timedelay_hist} shows the distribution of $\Delta t_{1j}$ ($j = 2, 3, 4$) with respect to the first-arriving image, obtained from the full LeR detectable population before applying the duty cycle and SNR cuts. The $\Delta t_{12}$ and $\Delta t_{13}$ distributions peak between days and weeks and span from minutes to years, consistent with the characteristic delays produced by galaxy-scale lenses in the geometric optics regime. The $\Delta t_{14}$ distribution, which corresponds to quadruply lensed events only, is shifted toward longer values because the last-arriving image typically emerges from a saddle point of the Fermat potential further from the central caustic\footnote{A caustic is a curve in the source plane where the lens magnification formally diverges; sources close to it are strongly magnified and produce closely spaced image pairs, while the last-arriving image of a quadruple system typically corresponds to a source located further from it, and hence more weakly magnified and more delayed.}. Delays below roughly one hour are rare, which works in favor of the joint analysis: the Earth usually has time to rotate enough between the two images to noticeably reorient the H1+L1 baseline relative to the source, giving the extra angular information exploited in \S\ref{sec:joint_PE_skyloc}.

\section{Pipeline overview}
\label{sec:pipeline_overview}


This chapter presents the PE pipeline built on the injection set described in Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation}, together with the automation infrastructure used to run it for all 157 events. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_diagram} places this pipeline within the full analysis developed in this thesis, from the population generation of Chapter~\ref{ch:simulation} (Stage~1) to the sky localization results of Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} (Stage~4). The rest of this section focuses on Stages~2 and~3: the automated generation of \textsc{Bilby}/\textsc{Hanabi} \texttt{.ini} files, and the single-image plus joint PE itself.

Starting from the filtered population \texttt{n\_lensed\_params\_bbh\_filtered.txt} produced in \S\ref{sec:injected_params} (Stage~1), a single script, \texttt{Generator\_inis\_lenskyloc.py}, generates for every event the two single-image \texttt{.ini} configurations, the joint \texttt{.ini} configuration, and the \href{https://slurm.schedmd.com/overview.html}{SLURM}\footnote{\url{https://slurm.schedmd.com/overview.html}} (Simple Linux Utility for Resource Management) scripts needed for the steps described below, populating priors and injection parameters directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} output (Stage~2, \S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}).

Each single-image \texttt{.ini} file is analyzed independently with \texttt{bilby\_pipe} \cite{bilby_pipe_paper}, using the lensed waveform source model provided by \textsc{Hanabi} (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}). This yields, for every event, two independent posterior distributions, one per image. Once both single-image jobs have completed, the joint \texttt{.ini} file is submitted with \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} \cite{PE_lensing_bayes}, which jointly analyzes the image pair through the shared-parameter likelihood described in \S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}, on top of the same \textsc{Bilby} inference framework used for the single-image runs.

From this point the pipeline splits into two independent post-processing tracks. In the first, since \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} does not compute the recovered SNR on its own, the joint posterior, together with the single-image data realizations, is passed to \texttt{hanabi\_\allowbreak postprocess\_\allowbreak result} (included in the \textsc{Hanabi} software package) with the \texttt{-{}-generate-snrs} option, which recomputes the single-image and network SNRs consistently across the single and joint analyses. A dedicated script then extracts these recovered SNRs into a summary table for each event, later combined across all events for the SNR consistency checks of \S\ref{sec:parameter_recovery}. In the second track, the single-image and joint posteriors are combined with \textsc{PESummary} \cite{PESummary} into a single metafile, from which sky maps are generated for each image and for the joint analysis using \href{https://lscsoft.docs.ligo.org/ligo.skymap/tool/ligo_skymap_from_samples.html}{\texttt{ligo-skymap-from-samples}}, \href{https://lscsoft.docs.ligo.org/ligo.skymap/tool/ligo_skymap_stats.html}{\texttt{ligo-skymap-stats}}, and \href{https://lscsoft.docs.ligo.org/ligo.skymap/tool/ligo_skymap_constellations.html}{\texttt{ligo-skymap-constellations}} \cite{SingerPrice2016, ligo_skymap_software}, providing the 90\% credible sky areas and the constellation localization compared between single-image and joint analyses in Chapter~\ref{Skyloc}.

The entire pipeline, from \texttt{.ini} generation to job submission and the SNR post-processing track, is automated on the \href{https://www.scbi.uma.es/web/es/supercomputacion/\#documentation}{Picasso HPC cluster}\footnote{\url{https://www.scbi.uma.es/web/es/supercomputacion/\#documentation}} (SLURM scheduler); the sky-map track is run separately, as described in \S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}.


\section{Single-image analysis with \textsc{Bilby}}
\label{sec:bilby_config}

As introduced in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}, each image is analyzed with \texttt{bilby\_pipe} as an isolated signal, characterized by its own data segment, noise realization, and set of extrinsic parameters. The \texttt{.ini} configuration is generated automatically for each image from a shared template (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}). Table~\ref{tab:bilby_settings} summarizes the detector and sampler settings common to all 157 events. The waveform generation frequency listed there, $13.33$\,Hz, is lower than the $f_{\min}=20$\,Hz of the analysis band: this is because the waveform generation frequency refers to the dominant $(\ell,m)=(2,2)$ mode, while a subdominant mode's instantaneous frequency scales as $f_{\ell m}=(m/2)f_{22}$, so starting the $(2,2)$ mode at $f_{22}=20/1.5=13.33$\,Hz ensures the $(3,3)$ mode, the most significant subdominant harmonic in this band, already has full content by $f_{\min}=20$\,Hz~\cite{higher_order_modes_waveform}.

The three parallel runs (\texttt{n-parallel}) are independent full \textsc{Dynesty} analyses combined into a single posterior to mitigate sampler-to-sampler variance, while \texttt{npool} parallelizes the likelihood evaluation within each individual run and matches the number of CPUs requested per SLURM job.

The same O5a PSD used to generate the population with \textsc{LeR} (\S\ref{sec:LER_population}) is adopted for the analysis, so that the noise assumptions are consistent between simulation and recovery. Unlike the data duration, which is fixed for the whole population in \textsc{LeR}, the segment duration adopted for each image is computed individually from its injected component masses and spins, using \textsc{LALSimulation}~\cite{lalsuite} bounds on the merger and ringdown time together with the expected waveform length at $f_{\min}=20$\,Hz, rounded up to the next power of two. This keeps each analysis as short as the signal allows without truncating the inspiral, rather than using a single conservative duration for the whole population.

The injected and recovered waveforms both use the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} approximant \cite{IMRPhenomXPNR}, rather than the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} approximant used by \textsc{LeR} to generate the population and compute the detection SNRs that define the selection process of \S\ref{sec:injected_params}. This choice is intentional: \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} is computationally cheap and sufficiently accurate for the population-level detectability calculation performed by \textsc{LeR}, whereas \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} incorporates precession effects calibrated against NR simulations and is the more accurate of the two, better suited for PE, following standard practice in recent LVK analyses. Indeed, the most recent O4 lensing search still uses \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} \cite{theligoscientificcollaboration2026gwtc40searchesgravitationalwavelensing}, while the standard (unlensed) LVK PE pipeline has already moved to \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} in GWTC-5 \cite{GWTC5_methods}. For the parameter ranges considered here, the two models are expected to agree closely, so this choice between the population-selection and PE stages is not expected to introduce a significant waveform-systematics effect.

The waveform is generated through \textsc{Hanabi}'s lensed source model, \href{https://github.com/ricokaloklo/hanabi/blob/master/hanabi/lensing/waveform.py}{\texttt{hanabi.\allowbreak lensing.\allowbreak waveform.\allowbreak strongly\_lensed\_BBH\_waveform}}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ricokaloklo/hanabi/blob/master/hanabi/lensing/waveform.py}}, which implements the lensed strain expression of \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}, combining the intrinsic \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} waveform with an image-dependent amplitude and Morse phase factor. In the single-image configuration this factor is fixed to \texttt{image\_type=1} (Type I, a minimum of the Fermat potential) rather than treated as a free parameter. Type I images carry no Morse phase shift ($n=0$, \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}), so their waveform is identical in phase to an ordinary unlensed signal; the only lensing imprint, an amplitude rescaling and a shifted arrival time, is already absorbed into the standard \texttt{luminosity\_distance} and \texttt{geocent\_time} parameters. The single-image prior fixes \texttt{image\_type} to this value regardless of the true injected type, so each image is analyzed as an ordinary CBC signal; identifying the true image type of each image, together with the relative magnification between them, is instead the specific role of the joint analysis (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), which samples \texttt{image\_type} independently for each image from a discrete prior.

The remaining priors follow standard choices for BBH PE: a chirp mass prior adapted individually for each event to a $\pm30\%$ range around its injected value (rather than a single wide range for the whole population, which would waste live points on regions of parameter space far from every event's true chirp mass), mass ratio and component-mass constraints, uniform spin magnitudes and precession angles, an isotropic sky location, and a $50$-$20\,000$\,Mpc uniform luminosity distance prior; the full \texttt{prior-dict} is reproduced in Appendix~$\S$\ref{implementataion_pipeline}.

This chirp mass prior can be centered on the injected value only because the true value is already known here; doing so also keeps each run computationally cheap by concentrating live points where the posterior actually has support, a non-negligible consideration given the total computational cost of the campaign (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}). A dedicated check across all 300 single-image chirp mass posteriors finds that only 4 (1.3\%) show noticeably more density right next to one prior edge than near the center of the range, and in every case the affected posterior belongs to the lower-SNR image of its pair, with single-image SNR of $7.2$ (\texttt{inj030}), $10.7$ (\texttt{inj104}), $15.8$ (\texttt{inj085}), and $10.3$ (\texttt{inj151}), among the lowest in the sample; for the remaining 296 posteriors the credible interval is unaffected by the prior boundary. For a real candidate, where the true value is not known, a wide prior would be required instead.

The spin magnitude priors are bounded to $a_{1,2}\in[0,0.99]$, whereas \textsc{LeR} draws the injected spins from $\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$ (Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}); an injected value above $0.99$ would therefore fall outside the recovery prior. Across all 157 injected events, the maximum injected spin magnitude is $0.78$ ($a_1$) and $0.80$ ($a_2$), so no event is affected by this truncation in practice.

\section{Joint analysis with \textsc{Hanabi}}
\label{sec:hanabi_joint}

As introduced in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}, the joint analysis is performed with \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe}, treating the two triggers of an event as two images of the same underlying source rather than as independent signals, with each trigger pointing to its corresponding single-image \texttt{.ini} file and otherwise inheriting its own data segment, noise realization, and PSD. The parameters shared between the two images are the intrinsic binary parameters (masses and spins), the sky location, the inclination, the polarization angle, and the coalescence phase, reflecting the fact that both images are gravitationally lensed copies of the same source. The luminosity distance, by contrast, is fitted independently for each image.

Formally, \textsc{Hanabi}'s lensing model requires specifying a relative magnification, an image type, and a time delay for each image pair. Table~\ref{tab:hanabi_lensing_priors} summarizes these lensing-specific priors: the relative magnification is fixed to $1$ for both images (not sampled), since its physical content is already captured by the two independently fitted luminosity distances discussed above, while the image type is the only genuinely free lensing-specific parameter, sampled independently for each image from a discrete uniform prior over the three Morse-index classes introduced in \S\ref{sec:strong_lensing}. The time delay is not assigned a dedicated lensing prior either: it is simply the difference between the two images' arrival times, each sampled with \textsc{Bilby}'s default $\pm0.1$\,s uniform \texttt{geocent\_time} prior around its own trigger time, the same as an ordinary, unlensed single-image analysis.

Sharing the sky location across two images observed at different times, typically separated by minutes to months (Figure~\ref{fig:timedelay_hist}), is the key mechanism behind the improvement in sky localization, as described in \S\ref{sec:joint_PE_skyloc}.

The joint likelihood is evaluated over both images simultaneously, using the same sampler settings as the single-image runs (Table~\ref{tab:bilby_settings}), but with substantially higher resource requirements, reflecting the added cost of a coherent likelihood evaluated across two detectors and two images: $180$\,GB of memory and a $71.5$\,h wall-time limit, compared to $110$\,GB and $48$\,h for each single-image run.


\section{Automation and infrastructure on Picasso}
\label{sec:automation_picasso}

Running this pipeline for all 157 events relies on two components: a script that generates every configuration and job file automatically, and a two-stage submission mechanism that enforces the job dependencies of \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}. Appendix~$\S$\ref{Reproducibility tutorial} complements this section with a practical, day-to-day guide to working on these two HPC clusters (SSH access, job monitoring, troubleshooting), aimed at future students rather than at reproducing this specific pipeline. Jobs execute directly inside a dedicated \texttt{conda}/\texttt{miniforge} environment (\href{https://github.com/XxavierGarcia/TFM_reproducibility_material/blob/main/environment_full.yml}{\texttt{hanabi\_bilby260}}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/XxavierGarcia/TFM_reproducibility_material/blob/main/environment_full.yml}}) on Picasso, whose exact specification is provided for reproducibility.

Summed over the sampling time reported in the logs of every parallel \textsc{Dynesty} run (\texttt{n-parallel\allowbreak =\allowbreak 3} per image or per joint analysis, each requesting 128 CPUs), the full PE campaign consumed approximately $830{,}000$ CPU-hours ($\sim\!6{,}500$ node-hours, or $\sim\!270$ node-days on 128-core nodes), split roughly evenly between the 942 single-image parallel runs and the 450 completed joint parallel runs. This scale of computational cost motivates several of the efficiency choices described below and in \S\ref{sec:bilby_config}, such as the per-event chirp-mass prior and the segment duration computed individually from each event's parameters, rather than adopting a single conservative configuration for the whole population.

\subsection{Configuration and script generation}

Beyond the duration and chirp-mass prior already computed for each event (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}), \texttt{Generator\_\allowbreak inis\_\allowbreak lenskyloc.py} populates the \texttt{injection-dict} of both single-image templates directly from the corresponding \textsc{LeR} parameters, mapping, e.g., \texttt{effective\_\allowbreak luminosity\_\allowbreak distance} (the magnification-rescaled apparent distance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:apparent_DL}) to \texttt{luminosity\_\allowbreak distance}, and \texttt{effective\_\allowbreak geocent\_\allowbreak time} (the lensed arrival time of Eq.~\eqref{eq:apparent_tc}) to \texttt{geocent\_time}, so that each image is injected with its own apparent extrinsic parameters rather than the source's true values; the same per-image mapping applies to \texttt{image\_type}, so each image is injected with its own true Morse type from \textsc{LeR} rather than a fixed value. Appendix~$\S$\ref{implementataion_pipeline} reproduces a representative single-image and joint \texttt{.ini} pair.

\subsection{Submission and job dependencies}

Job submission is split into two stages. The first, \texttt{submit\_all\_part1.sh}, submits the two single-image \texttt{bilby\_pipe} jobs for each event, followed by the joint \texttt{hanabi\_joint\_pipe} job with an explicit SLURM dependency on both single-image job IDs. The second stage, \texttt{submit\_all\_part2.sh}, runs after the first stage completes and launches the SNR-recovery track described in \S\ref{sec:pipeline_overview}. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_picasso} (Appendix~$\S$\ref{implementataion_pipeline}) lays out this Picasso (SLURM) branch of the pipeline in full, script by script.

The sky-map track, generating \textsc{PESummary} pages and sky maps for every event, is instead run on \href{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/guide/computing-centres/cit/}{LIGO's computing cluster at Caltech (CIT)}\footnote{\url{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/guide/computing-centres/cit/}} using \href{https://htcondor.readthedocs.io/en/latest/}{HTCondor}~\cite{HTCondor}, within the \href{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/conda/}{IGWN Conda Distribution}\footnote{\url{https://computing.docs.ligo.org/conda/}} (the \texttt{igwn-py311} series distributed via CVMFS, a rolling release updated periodically; the post-processing was run with the snapshot current at execution time): \texttt{Pesummaries\_\allowbreak automated\_\allowbreak condor\_\allowbreak all.py} and \texttt{Skymap\_\allowbreak condor.py} are each launched as an HTCondor job array of 157 processes, generating the summary pages and sky maps for all events in parallel. The PE itself, both single-image and joint, was always run on Picasso and never on CIT; only this post-processing track uses HTCondor, since the version of \textsc{PESummary} available in the Picasso environment produced errors, while the \texttt{igwn} environment on CIT ran it correctly. Because this track only produces summary pages and sky maps from posteriors already sampled on Picasso, the exact snapshot of the rolling \texttt{igwn} distribution used does not affect the scientific results; the relevant environment is the Picasso environment \texttt{hanabi\_\allowbreak bilby260} in which all sampling was performed. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_cit} (Appendix~$\S$\ref{implementataion_pipeline}) lays out this CIT (HTCondor) branch in the same level of detail. The complete pipeline code, including both the Picasso and CIT branches, is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/XxavierGarcia/TFM_reproducibility_material}.

\subsection{Incomplete events}
\label{sec:incomplete_events}

The joint analysis is the most expensive step of the pipeline, requiring up to $71.5$\,h of wall time per event (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}). Partway through the production run, the project's total compute allocation on Picasso was exhausted. New jobs could still be submitted afterward, but the project lost its scheduling priority and the maximum wall-time allowed per job dropped to $24$\,h, well below the $71.5$\,h a joint analysis needs to complete in a single run. In practice this meant that each remaining joint job would have had to be split across multiple $24$\,h segments, resumed from its checkpoint file each time (Appendix~$\S$\ref{Reproducibility tutorial}), while also queuing far longer than before due to the reduced priority; finishing the remaining joint analyses this way was no longer practical within the time available for this thesis. The last seven joint analyses\footnote{Events \texttt{inj110}, \texttt{inj115}, \texttt{inj118}, \texttt{inj137}, \texttt{inj138}, \texttt{inj142}, and \texttt{inj150}.} were left interrupted mid-run as a result. The next allocation period was not due to begin for approximately two months, and rather than delay the analysis by that much to recover seven events, it was finalized with the $150$ events that had completed successfully. This loss of $7$ out of $157$ events is a consequence of the compute allocation available at the time, not a scientific selection criterion, but it is not entirely random with respect to the injected parameters either: all seven incomplete events fall in the low end of the sample's chirp mass range ($11.3$-$20.0\,M_\odot$, against a full range of $10.9$-$79.7\,M_\odot$ and a median of $44.8\,M_\odot$), while their network SNR, time delay, and magnification are unremarkable compared to the rest of the sample. This is consistent with the origin of the cutoff: the data segment duration is computed individually per event from its masses (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}), and lower-mass systems require longer segments, so their joint analyses take longer and were more likely to still be running when the allocation was exhausted. So the final sample of 150 events has a small gap at low chirp mass compared to the full 157, caused by the analysis taking longer for these events, not by anything astrophysical.





\section{Parameter recovery and validation}
\label{sec:parameter_recovery}

This section evaluates how well the pipeline recovers the injected parameters of the 150 successfully analyzed events: first through a qualitative view of the recovered joint mass posteriors across the sample's SNR range, then through a direct comparison of the full joint and single-image posteriors for a few representative events (shown for the complete sample in Appendix~$\S$\ref{app:violins}), followed by systematic residual checks against the injected values for every recovered parameter, and finally through a quantitative look at the mass bias introduced by gravitational lensing, together with an attempt to correct it.

\subsection{Masses}

Figure~\ref{fig:mass1_mass2_recovery} shows the recovered $(m_1,m_2)$ 90\% credible contours for six representative events spanning the pair-combined network SNR range of the sample ($\rho_\mathrm{net}$ from $15.0$ to $65.3$, see Fig.~\ref{fig:mass_scatter} above), overlaid on the contours of the remaining events in grey. The two examples with the lowest SNR, \texttt{inj084} and \texttt{inj052}, show visibly elongated, curved contours: the leading-order phase evolution constrains mainly the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$, so individual masses remain only weakly determined along lines of near-constant $\mathcal{M}$, and this degeneracy widens as the SNR decreases. The shape of the contour, however, is not set by SNR alone: it also depends on how much of the signal is dominated by the inspiral. For lower mass systems, many PN cycles are observed in-band, and a high SNR is then enough to also break the mass-ratio degeneracy, recovering both individual masses precisely, as for \texttt{inj117} ($\rho_\mathrm{net}=65.3$). Higher-mass systems, by contrast, spend comparatively little time in the inspiral before merging within the band; there, a high SNR still pins down the total mass tightly through the merger and ringdown, but does not by itself supply the extra inspiral cycles needed to separate $m_1$ from $m_2$, so the mass ratio can remain comparatively broad even when the SNR is high. Masses are shown here in the detector frame $(1+z)m_i$, rather than the source frame reported in LVK catalogs \cite{GWTC5_methods}. In this analysis, each image's luminosity distance is sampled independently rather than sharing a common source distance across the pair, so what the posterior actually constrains for each image is the effective distance, $D_{L}^{\mathrm{eff}}=D_{L}^{\mathrm{true}}/\sqrt{|\mu|}$, consistent with how it was injected (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}). With only the two images' apparent distances, the true source distance $D_L^{\mathrm{true}}$ and the magnification $\mu$ of each image cannot be separated: two observed quantities constrain three unknowns, and additional information (an electromagnetic counterpart or an independent lens model) would be needed to break this degeneracy \cite{Poon2025_lens_reconstruction}. Converting the apparent distance directly to a redshift, and hence to a source-frame mass, would therefore absorb the unknown magnification with the true distance and bias the result; masses are reported in the detector frame instead to avoid this.

Before turning to the population-level residuals, it is instructive to look at the full recovered posteriors of individual events, and in particular at what the joint analysis adds over analyzing each image separately. Because the intrinsic binary parameters are shared between the two images in the joint analysis (\S\ref{sec:hanabi_joint}), the joint posterior draws on both data streams at once and can be narrower than either single-image posterior. Figure~\ref{fig:violin_excerpt} illustrates this for three representative events, showing the joint posterior (green) together with the two single-image posteriors (image 1 in red, image 2 in blue) and the injected value (black tick), for the mass ratio $q$, the effective spin $\chi_\mathrm{eff}$, and the effective luminosity distance $D_L^{\mathrm{eff}}$. For \texttt{inj152} the joint $90\%$ credible interval on $q$ is a factor of $1.7$ narrower than the better of the two single-image intervals, and for \texttt{inj113} the joint analysis tightens $\chi_\mathrm{eff}$ by a factor of $2.2$ and $D_L^{\mathrm{eff}}$ by a factor of $3.5$, the largest single improvement in the whole sample. The gain is not universal: for the high-SNR event \texttt{inj117}, already well measured from a single image, the joint and single-image posteriors nearly coincide across all three parameters. The improvement is therefore real but strongly event-dependent, largest where a single image constrains a parameter only weakly; the residual checks below quantify the recovery for the population as a whole. The equivalent figures for all 150 events and every recovered parameter, together with a quantitative ranking of these event-by-event improvements, are collected in Appendix~$\S$\ref{app:violins}, where a summary table lists the events with the largest gains alongside a few with little or no improvement.

That same ranking also includes a handful of events with $\mathrm{IF}_\theta<1$ for the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$ or the mass ratio $q$, meaning the joint posterior is broader than the better single-image posterior rather than narrower. This is not a problem with the joint analysis: it happens when a single high-SNR image happens to produce an anomalously narrow posterior for that parameter, and the joint analysis, required to stay consistent with both images at once, correctly returns a wider, more honest interval instead of inheriting that overconfidence. The clearest case is \texttt{inj085} ($\mathrm{IF}_q=0.80$): image~2's mass ratio posterior alone is narrower than the joint one, but the joint interval still recovers the injected value at least as reliably, so the lower $\mathrm{IF}_\theta$ here reflects a correction of an overconfident single-image result rather than a genuine loss of information.

For the remaining parameters, each event's joint posterior is summarized by its median ($\tilde{y}$) and 90\% credible interval (5th to 95th percentile), compared against the corresponding injected value from \textsc{LeR}. Figure~\ref{fig:residuals_mass} shows, across all 150 events, the residual between the posterior median and the injected value as a function of the injected value, with error bars spanning the 90\% credible interval, for the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$ (left) and mass ratio $q$ (right). The chirp mass residuals are centered on zero across the full mass range ($\tilde{y}=-0.002\,M_\odot$), consistent with $\mathcal{M}$ being the best determined parameter in the waveform phase. The mass ratio shows substantially more scatter ($\tilde{y}=-0.078$, $\sigma=0.114$), increasing with $q_\mathrm{inj}$ and biased toward lower recovered values for injected mass ratios near unity. This is a systematic trend, not random noise: because $\mathcal{M}$ is tightly measured but $q$ is not, different $(m_1,m_2)$ combinations that keep $\mathcal{M}$ fixed remain compatible with the data, so the recovered $q$ can drift from the injected value without moving $\mathcal{M}$. The consistent downward bias specifically for $q_\mathrm{inj}$ close to unity is a prior-boundary effect: since $q\leq1$ by the $m_1\geq m_2$ convention, posterior support that would otherwise extend above $q=1$ is reflected back below it, pulling the recovered median down whenever the true value sits close to this edge.

Recall from Fig.~\ref{fig:mass1_mass2_recovery} that the true source distance and the magnification of each image cannot be separated from the two apparent distances alone, which is why component masses were reported in the detector frame in the residual checks above.

The size of this bias can now be quantified directly: for each image, a naive source-frame chirp mass is computed by treating its effective luminosity distance as if it were the true source distance: converting it to a redshift under the same cosmology adopted by \textsc{LeR} ($H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, $\Omega_m=0.3$, flat $\Lambda$CDM, Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}), and dividing the (detector-frame) recovered component masses by $(1+z_{\mathrm{naive}})$.

Figure~\ref{fig:source_mass_bias_vs_injected} shows the resulting bias, $\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{source}}_{\mathrm{naive}}-\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{source}}_{\mathrm{inj}}$, against the injected source-frame chirp mass, for image 1 (left) and image 2 (right). The bias is systematically positive and an order of magnitude larger than the chirp mass measurement noise seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:residuals_mass} ($\sigma\approx2\,M_\odot$ there, versus a median of $11.6\,M_\odot$ and $11.4\,M_\odot$ here for images 1 and 2), so this is not simply propagated distance measurement noise.

Figure~\ref{fig:snr_recovery_consistency} closes this residual comparison with a quantity that is not itself a source parameter but a consistency check on the pipeline: the recovered network SNR $\rho_\mathrm{post}$ against the injected network SNR $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$. Because the joint analysis combines both lensed images, $\rho_\mathrm{post}$ is itself a two-image quantity, the median of $\sqrt{\rho_\mathrm{net,1}^2+\rho_\mathrm{net,2}^2}$ recomputed from the joint posterior samples (\S\ref{sec:automation_picasso}); like $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$, this is the optimal SNR evaluated for the recovered template rather than a matched-filter SNR against the noisy data, so no random noise-realization scatter is expected between the two, only the systematic effects discussed below. For a consistent comparison, $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$ is defined here as the quadrature sum of the two per-image network SNRs reported by \textsc{LeR} for that event, $\rho_\mathrm{inj}=\sqrt{\rho_\mathrm{net,1,inj}^2+\rho_\mathrm{net,2,inj}^2}$, rather than the SNR of either image alone. With this definition the residual is centered close to zero and shows no systematic trend with $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$ ($\tilde{y}=-0.335$). Its scatter, however, is substantial ($\sigma=2.89$), well above the few-percent level expected between two optimal-SNR estimates of the same signals, and is dominated by a single pronounced outlier, \texttt{inj113}, at $\rho_\mathrm{post}-\rho_\mathrm{inj}=+18.3$. This clear discrepancy led us to look more closely at how the injected SNR is computed by \textsc{LeR}.

The outlier, \texttt{inj113}, sits in an extreme corner of the parameter space: an extreme magnification ($|\mu_1|\approx491$ and $|\mu_2|\approx604$, the third-highest in the sample and within the $|\mu|\sim10^3$ tail of Fig.~\ref{fig:magnification_hist}) that boosts its effective SNR, combined with a high total mass ($M_\mathrm{det}\approx136\,M_\odot$, $72$nd percentile). \texttt{gwsnr}'s interpolation estimator reproduces the exact inner-product SNR to better than $0.5\%$ on average across randomly sampled parameters \cite{gwsnrpythonpackageefficient}, but it is built on the inspiral-based FINDCHIRP amplitude scaling, $\rho_\mathrm{opt}\propto\mathcal{M}^{5/6}/D_\mathrm{eff}$ \cite{gwsnrpythonpackageefficient}, and is expected to be least accurate for high-total-mass, merger-dominated systems such as this one, where much of the SNR accumulates in the merger and ringdown rather than the inspiral. This suggests the injected SNR, rather than the recovery, as the origin of the discrepancy.

To confirm this, $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$ was recomputed for the full sample with \texttt{gwsnr}'s exact estimator (\texttt{snr\_type=\allowbreak "inner\_\allowbreak product"}), which evaluates the noise-weighted frequency-domain inner product directly instead of interpolating a precomputed grid. For \texttt{inj113} this raises the injected network SNR from $38.9$ to $59.7$, an underestimate of about $35\%$ by the interpolation, far larger than its sub-percent average accuracy and confirming that this event is an extreme corner of the method rather than a generic failure. The exact value is in close agreement with the recovered $\rho_\mathrm{post}=57.2$ and reduces the residual from $+18.3$ to $-2.5$, turning a $6\sigma$ outlier into an ordinary event. Across the whole sample, the exact recomputation removes the outlier entirely (the largest absolute residual drops from $18.3$ to $6.6$), roughly halves the scatter ($\sigma$ from $2.89$ to $1.43$), and tightens the median toward zero ($-0.34$ to $-0.06$), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:snr_recovery_exact}. This confirms that the large scatter and the \texttt{inj113} outlier in Fig.~\ref{fig:snr_recovery_consistency} originate in the accuracy limitation of \textsc{LeR}'s fast interpolation SNR estimator, not in the joint PE recovery. The residual that remains after the exact recomputation ($\sigma=1.43$) is at the few-percent level and is consistent with two genuine, smaller differences between the two calculations: \texttt{gwsnr} integrates the SNR up to a default $2048\,\mathrm{Hz}$, wider than the $896\,\mathrm{Hz}$ upper cutoff of the \textsc{Bilby} analysis (Table~\ref{tab:bilby_settings}), and the population is injected with the \texttt{IMRPhenomXPHM} waveform while the recovery uses \texttt{IMRPhenomXPNR} (\S\ref{sec:bilby_config}), two models expected to agree closely but not exactly for these systems.

Importantly, this SNR mismatch does not affect any of the results of this thesis. The injected SNR computed by \textsc{LeR} enters the analysis only through the $\rho_\mathrm{net}\geq10$ detectability selection (\S\ref{sec:injected_params}) and as the reference for this consistency check; it is never itself an input to the PE, which operates directly on the injected waveform and detector noise rather than on any scalar SNR value. The interpolation inaccuracy, moreover, appears only at high SNR, far above the $\rho_\mathrm{net}=10$ threshold, so it does not change which events pass the selection. The recovered parameters and the sky localization results of Chapter~\ref{Skyloc} therefore depend only on the full PE of the injected data and are unaffected by the accuracy of \textsc{LeR}'s fast SNR estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:snr_recovery_consistency} is retained with the interpolation-based $\rho_\mathrm{inj}$ precisely because it is this quantity, not the exact one, that defined the injected population.

\subsection{Recovering the true source-frame mass with an assumed lens model}
\label{sec:lens_model_recovery}

Recovering the true source distance, and hence the true source-frame mass, requires resolving the magnification-distance degeneracy identified above. For a general lens model this is not possible from the two apparent distances alone: even a full reconstruction of the lens from all its lensed-image observables leaves residual degeneracies, such as an overall similarity transformation, that can only be broken with electromagnetic follow-up of the identified lens galaxy (its redshift and Einstein radius, plus an independent mass estimate from its velocity dispersion) \cite{Poon2025_lens_reconstruction}, or in the presence of wave-optics lensing effects, which imprint interference patterns on the waveform that break this degeneracy without requiring an electromagnetic counterpart \cite{Cremonese2021_wave_optics_degeneracy}. Since this is a simulation-only study, no such follow-up exists, and substituting \textsc{LeR}'s known lens truth in its place would be circular, recovering the injected value by construction rather than testing a method.

Instead, the degeneracy is broken by assuming a simple, generic lens model \cite{Kim2024_lensing_distance}, reducing the unknowns to a single free parameter: the normalized source-lens separation $u\equiv\beta/\theta_E$ introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:PM_magnification}. In the geometric optics limit, the magnifications of the two images of a point-mass (PM) or singular isothermal sphere (SIS) lens are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{PM:}&\quad \mu_+(u) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}} + 1\right),\ \ |\mu_-(u)| = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}} - 1\right), \\
\mathrm{SIS:}&\quad \mu_+(u) = 1+\frac{1}{u},\ \ |\mu_-(u)| = \frac{1}{u}-1 \quad (u<1).
\end{split}
\label{eq:lens_mu}
\end{equation}
Both expressions are written in terms of $|\mu_-|$ rather than the signed value of Eq.~\eqref{eq:PM_magnification}, since only the magnitude of the magnification enters the apparent distance and hence the observable relative magnification below. The relative magnification $\mu_{\mathrm{rel}}=|\mu_-|/\mu_+$ is measurable directly from the two apparent distances already in the joint posterior, without any external information,
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\mathrm{rel}} = \left(\frac{D_{L,+}^{\mathrm{eff}}}{D_{L,-}^{\mathrm{eff}}}\right)^2,
\label{eq:mu_rel}
\end{equation}
where the ``+'' image is defined, per event, as the one with the smaller apparent distance. Equations~\eqref{eq:lens_mu} and \eqref{eq:mu_rel} are inverted numerically to solve for $u$, which is then substituted back into the corresponding expression for $\mu_+(u)$ to obtain a corrected true distance $D_L=\sqrt{\mu_+}\,D_{L,+}^{\mathrm{eff}}$, from which the source-frame chirp mass is recomputed as in the naive case above.

Figure~\ref{fig:source_mass_recovery} shows the resulting bias for both models, evaluated with only the joint posterior's own apparent distances, never the injected truth. Unlike the naive bias, which is positive for every event, the corrected bias takes both signs, so the median alone can partly reflect cancellation between events corrected too much and events corrected too little, rather than a genuine reduction. The median absolute bias, $\widetilde{|y|}$, avoids this and is used here as the main summary statistic: the naive bias corresponds to $\widetilde{|y|}\approx11.5\,M_\odot$ (Figure~\ref{fig:source_mass_bias_vs_injected}, where every residual has the same sign), the point-mass correction reduces this to $\widetilde{|y|}=6.18\,M_\odot$ ($\tilde{y}=5.86$, $\sigma=6.33$), and the SIS correction reduces it further to $\widetilde{|y|}=4.34\,M_\odot$ ($\tilde{y}=3.52$, $\sigma=5.62$).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/source_mass_recovery.png}
    \caption[Source-frame chirp mass bias after lens-model correction]{Bias of the source-frame chirp mass after correcting for magnification with an assumed point-mass (\textit{left}) or SIS (\textit{right}) lens model, against the injected source-frame chirp mass, for all 150 events.}
    \label{fig:source_mass_recovery}
\end{figure}

The SIS correction outperforms the point-mass correction on both statistics, consistent with \textsc{LeR}'s true lens model being an EPL profile with external shear (Table~\ref{tab:LER_params}, $\gamma\sim\mathcal{N}(2.0,0.2^2)$), structurally closer, on average, to an isothermal profile than to a point mass. Neither model brings the bias down to the noise level seen in the detector-frame chirp mass residual ($\sigma\approx2\,M_\odot$, Figure~\ref{fig:residuals_mass}); what remains comes from the difference between the simple, round lens assumed here and the true elliptical, sheared lens of each event.

\subsection{Confirming the lensed nature of the injected pairs}
\label{sec:coherence_ratio_results}

The coherence ratio $\mathcal{C}_\mathrm{U}^\mathrm{L}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:coherence_ratio}, \S\ref{sec:lensing_bayes_factor}) tests whether the joint analysis is favored over treating the two images as independent sources, and can be evaluated directly from the signal-vs-noise Bayes factor already reported by each \textsc{Bilby} and \textsc{Hanabi} run, without any additional computation, using Eq.~\eqref{eq:coherence_ratio_practical}. For each of the 150 successfully analyzed pairs (\S\ref{sec:incomplete_events}), $\ln\mathcal{C}_\mathrm{U}^\mathrm{L}$ is computed from the $\ln\mathcal{B}_{SN}$ values of the two single-image runs and the joint run. Table~\ref{tab:coherence_ratio} summarizes the resulting distribution, and Figure~\ref{fig:coherence_ratio_hist} shows it in full. \section{From posterior samples to sky maps}
\label{sec:skymap_generation}

The sky position posteriors obtained from each PE run are converted into all-sky probability maps using \texttt{ligo-skymap-from-samples}, which performs an adaptive kernel density estimate on the right ascension and declination samples and produces a multi-resolution HEALPix \cite{Gorski2005} map. For each of the 150 successfully analyzed lensed pairs (\S\ref{sec:incomplete_events}), three sky maps are generated: one for each single-image analysis (image~1 and image~2 independently) and one for the joint analysis. From each map, the 50\% and 90\% credible areas are extracted with \texttt{ligo-skymap-stats}, following the definition of the minimum credible region $\mathcal{R}_{90}$ given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sky_area}. The dominant constellation and its contained probability fraction are obtained with \texttt{ligo-skymap-constellations} (\S\ref{sec:constellation}).

Figure~\ref{fig:skymap_example} illustrates the effect of the joint analysis on sky localization for a representative event (injection~002, $\Delta t \approx 5.6$\,h between images). The first single-image analysis yields a broad arc spanning $A_{90} \approx 970\,\mathrm{deg}^2$, characteristic of a two-detector network where the source direction is constrained to an arc of constant time delay between H1 and L1 (\S\ref{sec:single_image_skyloc}). For this particular event, the second image happens to be more strongly magnified than the first ($|\mu_2|\approx129$ versus $|\mu_1|\approx80$) and therefore has higher SNR; its 90\% area is correspondingly smaller, $\approx 416\,\mathrm{deg}^2$, and concentrated in a single region rather than an extended arc. This is a property of this specific pair rather than a general rule, since either image could equally be the more magnified one. The joint posterior, which requires both images to share the same sky position (Eq.~\eqref{eq:joint_evidence_lensed}), collapses the credible region to $\approx 27\,\mathrm{deg}^2$: a factor of $\sim\!36$ improvement over the broader single image and $\sim\!15$ over the narrower one. This large reduction, well beyond what either image achieves on its own regardless of its individual SNR, confirms the mechanism described in \S\ref{sec:joint_PE_skyloc}: the $5.6$\,h time delay is long enough to significantly reorient the H1-L1 antenna pattern between the two images, so combining them breaks a degeneracy that improving either image alone could not.

  \subsection{90\% credible area}
  \label{sec:area_comparison}

The 90\% credible sky area $A_{90}$ is computed for each of the 150 events, separately for the best single-image analysis (defined as $\min(A_{90}^{\mathrm{img1}},\,A_{90}^{\mathrm{img2}})$) and for the joint analysis. The improvement factor per event is defined as $\mathrm{IF}_{A_{90}} \equiv A_{90}^{\mathrm{best\,single}} / A_{90}^{\mathrm{joint}}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:cdf_area} shows the cumulative and probability density distributions of $A_{90}$ for the best single-image and joint analyses. The joint distribution is shifted to smaller areas across the entire range. The median 90\% area drops from $571\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ (best single image) to $99.9\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ (joint), a ratio of medians of $5.7$. The median per-event improvement factor is $5.1$, confirming that the improvement is not driven by a small number of outliers but is systematic across the population. The joint analysis improves the sky localization for all 150 events without exception ($\mathrm{IF}_{A_{90}} > 1$ in every case), with individual improvement factors ranging from $1.1$ to $28.0$. Five events ($3.3\%$) reach a joint $A_{90} < 10\,\mathrm{deg}^2$, while half of the sample ($75/150$) falls below $100\,\mathrm{deg}^2$. The $10\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ threshold is commonly adopted as a benchmark for host galaxy identification and multimessenger follow-up with wide-field survey telescopes \cite{Abbott2020Prospects}: sky areas at or below this scale contain a manageable number of candidate host galaxies for spectroscopic follow-up, and can be covered by a small number of pointings with instruments such as DECam, ZTF, or the Rubin Observatory.

  \subsection{Constellation localization}
  \label{sec:constellation}

The \texttt{ligo-skymap-constellations} tool \cite{SingerPrice2016} identifies the IAU constellation that contains the largest fraction of the posterior probability from each sky map. Unlike the credible area of \S\ref{sec:area_comparison}, this metric maps the localization onto a recognizable region of the sky rather than a raw solid angle, which is useful for visualization and outreach, and, as discussed below, offers a possible naming convention for lensed pairs. A caveat of this metric is that the 88 IAU constellations span a wide range of angular sizes, from $\sim\!70$ to $\sim\!1300\,\mathrm{deg}^2$, so the constellation fraction mixes together how well localized a source is with where on the sky it happens to fall; the comparison with real GWTC-5 events below, which is subject to the same effect, provides a control for this dependence.

Figure~\ref{fig:constellation_vs_snr} shows the probability fraction contained in the dominant constellation as a function of network SNR. For single-image analyses, the median fraction in the top constellation is $\sim\!41\%$ (best of the two images), with only $50/150$ events exceeding $50\%$. The joint analysis raises the median to $68.6\%$, with $115/150$ events ($76.7\%$) above $50\%$ and $47/150$ ($31.3\%$) above $90\%$. The clustering of joint results toward high constellation fractions, visible in the upper part of the scatter plot, reflects the same mechanism as the sky area reduction: the joint posterior concentrates into a compact region that fits within a single constellation boundary.

A high constellation fraction also has a practical use beyond visualization: naming lensed pairs. Lensed image pairs are currently identified by concatenating the two individual event names (e.g.\ GW200105-GW200115), a notation that gets harder to read as more pairs are found, particularly when their events share similar dates. Constellation-based names are already the default convention in several branches of astronomy, and a comparable scheme could in principle be adopted for confidently localized lensed pairs. The $47/150$ joint analyses reaching above $90\%$ found here suggest this is not a marginal possibility: for a non-negligible fraction of events, the joint posterior is confidently confined to a single constellation. This fraction is expected to improve substantially if the analysis is extended to a three-detector network (H1, L1, and Virgo), which would further tighten the joint localization.

This comparison highlights the localization advantage that a third detector provides in standard analyses, and underscores the value of the joint lensed-image approach: even with only two detectors, the joint analysis achieves constellation fractions (median $68.6\%$) that are comparable to those of the three-detector network on real data.

\subsection{Comparison with the literature}
\label{sec:literature_comparison}

Two recent studies have investigated sky localization improvements from strongly lensed GW images, using complementary approaches.

Hannuksela et al. \cite{hubble_constant_lensing} demonstrated that quadruply lensed GW events observed by the full LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA network can be localized to one or a few candidate host galaxies by combining GW sky maps with EM constraints on the lens galaxy properties, ultimately achieving sub-arcsecond precision within the identified host. Their approach relies on the availability of four images and three or more detectors, and targets a different regime from the present work: galaxy-scale identification rather than population-level sky area statistics.

Li and Hannuksela \cite{Hannuksela_skyloc} performed the first systematic study of repeated-signal localization using \textsc{Bayestar}~\cite{SingerPrice2016} sky maps on a simulated lensed population observed with the three-detector HLV network (H1, L1, and Virgo), adopting \textsc{Bilby}'s default detector sensitivity curves (the Advanced LIGO O4 and Advanced Virgo design PSDs). They found that combining two images reduces the 90\% credible area by roughly an order of magnitude, somewhat larger than the median improvement factor of $5.1$ found in this work. The key differences with respect to the present analysis are: (i) this work uses a two-detector network (H1+L1) with the projected O5a PSD, rather than the three-detector HLV network with \textsc{Bilby}'s default design curves, (ii) the localization is based on full Bayesian PE with \textsc{Bilby} and \textsc{Hanabi} rather than the rapid \textsc{Bayestar} algorithm, and (iii) the population is generated with \textsc{LeR} including a realistic duty cycle filter. The smaller improvement factor obtained here is expected: with only two detectors, the single-image localization is already less constrained by triangulation and relies more heavily on antenna pattern information, so the additional gain from combining a second image, which acts through the same antenna pattern channel, is comparatively smaller than in a three-detector network where the two images can each contribute additional triangulation baselines. Despite this difference in magnitude, both studies converge on the same qualitative conclusion: combining two lensed images produces a substantial, consistent improvement in sky localization across detector configurations and analysis methods.

\section{Summary of sky localization results}
\label{sec:skyloc_summary}

The results presented in this chapter demonstrate that the joint PE analysis of strongly lensed BBH image pairs produces a systematic and substantial improvement in sky localization compared to the analysis of individual images, even with a two-detector network. The median 90\% credible area drops from $571\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ (best single image) to $99.9\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ (joint), corresponding to a median per-event improvement factor of $5.1$. The improvement is universal across the sample: all 150 events are better localized by the joint analysis, with improvement factors ranging from $1.1$ to $28.0$. Five events ($3.3\%$) reach a joint sky area below the $10\,\mathrm{deg}^2$ threshold commonly adopted for host galaxy identification and multimessenger follow-up \cite{Abbott2020Prospects}, placing them within reach of targeted EM searches with wide-field survey telescopes. Comparison with real GWTC-5 events shows that the joint lensed analysis with two detectors achieves constellation localization fractions comparable to those of the three-detector network, and comparison with prior theoretical work confirms that combining two lensed images produces a substantial and consistent sky localization gain across different detector configurations and analysis methods, albeit with a magnitude that depends on the detector network considered.
